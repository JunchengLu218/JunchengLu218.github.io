Juncheng Lu

I'm a master's student in Cognitive Science in Education at Teachers College, Columbia University, and a research assistant in the Social Cognitive and Neural Sciences Lab with Dr. Jon Freeman and Postdoctoral Research Scientist Dr. Samuel A. W. Klein.

Before Columbia I received my B.S. in Psychology from Beijing Normal University, where I worked with Dr. Xiaochun Han on the cognitive and neural mechanisms of social decision-making.

I study how people make social decisions, using evidence-accumulation and process-dissociation models. In some projects I relate model parameters to EEG and fMRI; in others I ask which latent decision processes people can monitor. My current work focuses on harm decisions, racial bias in speeded choice, and metacognition of decision bias.

Research

Computational and Neural Mechanisms of Collateral-Harm Decisions

Juncheng Lu*, Y. Zhang*, X. Han, Z. Xiao, X. Han† (2026)

Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, revise & resubmit

Two studies (total N = 983) pairing a cross-cultural behavioral experiment with EEG. Hierarchical DDMs and P2/N2 ERP components relate civilian race and pain expression to decision caution: Observed a Race × Expression interaction in neural signals associated with decision caution despite no parallel condition differences in overt attack choices; I led study design, data collection, and analyses, and contributed to manuscript drafting and revision.

Computational modeling of speeded weapon-versus-tool decisions under racial priming

Freeman Lab · Sept – Dec 2025

HDDM and MPT models fit to speeded weapon-versus-tool decisions under racial priming to examine how evidence accumulation and latent processing components contribute to biased decisions. Competing cognitive architectures were compared, and model-derived parameters related to fMRI activation to evaluate accounts of threat processing and cognitive control.

Metacognition of predictions bias on the Implicit Association Test

First author

Manuscript in preparation

How accurately can people predict their own IAT decision biases? Across 2,986 observations from 1,866 participants on four race and age IATs, I use hierarchical MPT/Quad and HDDM models to test which latent processes track metacognitive accuracy.

Cognitive and neural mechanisms of discriminated-attack decisions in intergroup conflicts

Y. Zhang*, Juncheng Lu*, X. Han† (2026)

Manuscript in preparation

d Four studies on how social-role information shapes civilian–soldier differences in attack decisions, bystander judgments, and representations of warmth and competence. Logistic and linear mixed-effects models over participants and social roles show that the civilian–soldier difference shrinks once role information is available, across both decision and representation tasks.
